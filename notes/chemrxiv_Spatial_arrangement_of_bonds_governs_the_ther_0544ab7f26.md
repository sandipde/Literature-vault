## Summary
Multivalency, the simultaneous engagement of many weak bonds, underlies recognition between cells, viruses, and proteins and enables assembly of DNA origami into micrometer-scale structures. Predicting the stability of a multivalent interface is difficult because each bond's contribution depends on the number, strength, and arrangement of the other bonds and on the scaffold that presents them, which rarely vary independently. We use DNA origami tiles with 11 addressable sites along one edge to build 236 interfaces in which bond number, strength, and arrangement are set independently, and measure their melting by FRET. Multivalency stabilizes interfaces far beyond any single bond. Once two tiles dock, each further bond finds its partner at an effective concentration about 105-fold above the tile concentration. Stability nevertheless grows subadditively, because only part of an interface melts as one unit and added bonds contribute only insofar as they are formed near the interface melting temperature. Arrangement matters as much as identity: interfaces built from the same bonds lose stability as bonds spread apart, and moving one bond away from two fixed bonds costs 2.5–3 °C per skipped site. A three-parameter multivalent model in which each bond’s effective concentration decays with its distance from the others predicts held-out melting temperatures to within 1.3 °C, outperforming a gradient-boosted regressor. Coarse-grained simulations of polymer-tethered binders reproduce this loss and show that linker and scaffold rigidity set how well binding sites stay in register. These results give design rules and a predictive tool for multicomponent DNA assembly and other multivalent systems.
