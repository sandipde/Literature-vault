## Summary
Words sound different every time they are said, both by the same person and across people. This variability has been found to have mixed effects on learning. For example, 14-month-olds benefit from talker variability when learning minimal pairs, but struggle when learning distinct-sounding words. Talker variability is found to be particularly beneficial for learning to produce novel words. All of these previous studies have used passive exposure, presenting participants with predetermined orders or fixed types of variability. Here, we used an active learning paradigm wherein preschoolers could choose who to hear words from during word learning. Then, we tested whether participants seek out variability during word learning, how these talker variability choices influence word learning and whether actively making these choices results in different outcomes than passively receiving them. We find that preschoolers choose to hear words from multiple talkers, and talker variability choices interact with active learning to predict production, but not comprehension. Taken together, our results suggest that preschool aged children can make choices are benefit their own learning, informing theories of both talker variability and active learning.
